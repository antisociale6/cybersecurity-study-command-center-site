Beginner Web Service Threat Model Example

Start with a fictional or locally owned service. Map actors, components, data stores, flows, and trust boundaries; connect plausible misuse scenarios to specific assets; then record controls, owners, residual uncertainty, and evidence needed for validation.

Curriculum activity: Threat-Model a Small Web Service

This note helps you create: Design-Stage Threat Model for a Local Appointment Service

A practical, evidence-led method

  1. Describe a fictional or locally owned service, its users, business purpose, and the decisions the model should inform.
  2. Map components, data stores, external dependencies, actors, and data flows at a level a reviewer can understand.
  3. Mark trust boundaries and identify where identity, sensitive information, or administrative authority changes hands.
  4. Write plausible misuse or failure scenarios and connect each scenario to a specific asset and boundary.
  5. Document proposed controls, residual uncertainty, an accountable owner, and evidence needed for later validation.

Safety boundary

Keep the exercise at design-review level and use a fictional or owned local service. Do not probe public services, enumerate real users, or include credentials or personal information.

Copy-ready artifact outline

Start a document named Design-Stage Threat Model for a Local Appointment Service and use these five sections. Replace every prompt with evidence from your owned, synthetic, or explicitly authorized lab.

  1. Service scope and decision

    Describe the fictional or local service, intended users, business purpose, protected assets, exclusions, and the design decision this model should inform.

  2. Component and data-flow map

    List actors, components, data stores, external dependencies, and numbered data flows so another learner can redraw the system without guessing.

  3. Trust-boundary register

    For each boundary, record what crosses it, which identity or authority changes, the expected protection, and the evidence still needed.

  4. Misuse and failure scenarios

    Connect each plausible scenario to one asset, component, flow, and boundary. Record impact, existing control, uncertainty, and a safe validation idea.

  5. Control and validation backlog

    Prioritize proposed controls by decision value, owner, residual risk, and the local evidence required before the design can be accepted.

Before you call it complete

  • Authorization and scope are visible.
  • Every conclusion points to evidence or is labeled as an assumption.
  • Sensitive details are removed or redacted.
  • Cleanup, rollback, and remaining limitations are recorded.

What to prove

Aim to produce a reviewable artifact such as Design-Stage Threat Model for a Local Appointment Service. Keep facts, assumptions, limits, and redactions visible so a reviewer can understand what the work demonstrates and what it does not.

Primary references

Check your decisions against these official sources. Record the version or access date you used because guidance can change.

  • OWASP Threat Modeling Project

    Use its maintained four-question workflow to scope the service, identify failure scenarios, choose mitigations, and validate the finished model.

  • Microsoft Security Development Lifecycle (SDL)

    Use its design guidance to map components and data flows, expose trust relationships, prioritize threats, and track mitigations.
